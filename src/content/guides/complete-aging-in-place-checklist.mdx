## Direct answer

If you are helping an older adult stay safer at home, start with the areas where small changes can reduce obvious risks quickly: clear walking paths, bathroom safety, lighting, medications, emergency contacts, and ways to call for help. Then move room by room and write down anything that needs a professional opinion.

The goal is not to make the home perfect in one day. The goal is to reduce obvious hazards, organize essential information, and create a practical plan for what to fix next.

## Why this matters

Falls are not an inevitable part of aging, but they are a serious threat to health and independence. CDC states that falls can be prevented and identifies older-adult falls as a major injury and emergency-care issue. Use that as the guiding frame: reduce obvious risks, organize help, and involve qualified professionals when the situation is bigger than a checklist.

## Safety note

This guide is for general education and organization. It is not medical, legal, financial, construction, or individualized safety advice. For decisions involving health, medications, mobility, home modifications, legal authority, or personal safety, talk with a qualified professional.

## What to fix first

Start with high-impact basics:

- Clear the main walking paths.
- Remove loose clutter from floors.
- Improve lighting in hallways, stairs, bathrooms, bedrooms, and entryways.
- Check for loose rugs, curled rug edges, cords, and low furniture that could create trip hazards.
- Make sure frequently used items are reachable without climbing.
- Confirm the older adult can call for help from the bedroom, bathroom, and main living area.
- Put emergency contact information somewhere easy to find.
- Make sure trusted helpers know how to access emergency information and supplies.

## Room-by-room checklist

### Bathroom

- Check for slippery tub or shower surfaces.
- Check whether the path to the bathroom is well lit at night.
- Keep toiletries easy to reach.
- Notice whether the person relies on towel bars, counters, or shower doors for support.
- Consider whether a shower chair, transfer bench, raised toilet seat, or properly installed grab bars should be discussed with a professional.
- Check whether bath mats create a trip hazard.
- Keep the floor dry and uncluttered.

### Bedroom

- Keep a clear path from bed to bathroom.
- Make lighting reachable from bed.
- Keep a phone, alert device, or call option reachable from bed.
- Review whether the bed height is manageable.
- Keep glasses, hearing aids, cane, walker, slippers, and other essentials in consistent places.
- Remove cords, rugs, or clutter near the bed.
- Consider whether motion lighting would help at night.

### Bed rail caution

Adult portable bed rails can help some people with support or movement in bed, but they can also create serious entrapment and fall hazards. CPSC and FDA both warn that bed rails should be used carefully, checked for recalls and compatibility, installed according to instructions, and evaluated for whether they are appropriate for the person and situation.

### Kitchen

- Store frequently used items between waist and shoulder height.
- Reduce the need for step stools.
- Check whether appliance controls are easy to read and use.
- Keep floors dry and clear.
- Store heavy pots, dishes, and appliances safely.
- Create a routine for food expiration, groceries, and meal preparation.
- Treat repeated cooking concerns as a planning topic, not a simple organization problem.

### Stairs, hallways, and entryways

- Check stair lighting.
- Check whether stair edges are easy to see.
- Check whether handrails are secure and easy to grip.
- Keep stairs free of clutter.
- Check thresholds and entryways for trip hazards.
- Make porch, driveway, and walkway lighting adequate.
- Discuss railings, ramps, or threshold modifications with a professional when needed.

## Caregiver organization checklist

Create a simple caregiver binder or folder with:

- Emergency contacts
- Doctors and specialists
- Pharmacy information
- Current medication list
- Allergies and adverse reactions
- Diagnoses or major medical history
- Insurance information
- Preferred hospital
- Home access instructions
- Appointment notes
- Care schedule
- Family roles and responsibilities

Ready.gov recommends that older adults and caregivers assess needs, make a plan, build a support network, and include essentials such as medicines, medical supplies, chargers, and copies of important cards in emergency planning.

## When to call a professional

Consider professional help if:

- There has been a recent fall or near-fall.
- The person struggles with stairs, transfers, bathing, toileting, or walking paths.
- Medication mistakes are happening.
- Memory, wandering, judgment, or cooking safety concerns are present.
- A major modification is being considered.
- A ramp, stair lift, grab bar, shower change, bed rail, or mobility device is needed.
- The person is coming home after a hospital or rehab stay.
- Family members disagree about whether the home is still safe.
- The older adult lives alone and cannot reliably call for help.

Potential helpers may include a primary care clinician, pharmacist, occupational therapist, physical therapist, certified aging-in-place specialist, geriatric care manager, licensed contractor, elder law attorney, or local aging-services agency depending on the issue.

## Finding local support

The Administration for Community Living operates the Eldercare Locator, a public service that connects older adults and caregivers with local support resources. Families can use it to find local aging services, transportation support, home-delivered meals, caregiver resources, and other community-based help.
