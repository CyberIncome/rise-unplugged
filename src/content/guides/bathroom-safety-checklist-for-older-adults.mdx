## Direct answer

Bathroom safety starts with the places where wet surfaces, transfers, low lighting, and unstable support can create problems. Review the path to the bathroom, tub or shower surface, toilet transfers, lighting, reach zones, and whether any support equipment should be evaluated by a qualified professional.

The practical goal is simple: reduce obvious hazards, stop relying on unstable fixtures for support, and identify which changes require professional help.

## Why the bathroom deserves its own checklist

Falls are a major safety concern for older adults, and CDC recommends home-safety steps such as removing trip hazards, improving lighting, and installing grab bars in bathrooms. NIAMS also lists bathroom grab bars, bathmats, and shower seating with nonskid leg tips among simple home safety improvements that can help reduce fall risk.

Bathing and toileting are also dignity and independence issues. A nationally representative study of adults 65 and older found substantial unmet need for equipment such as grab bars and seats among people with bathing or toileting difficulty, and described these low-technology supports as a way to reduce the mismatch between a person's capabilities and the demands of the home environment.

## Safety note

This guide is general education. It is not individualized safety, medical, occupational therapy, construction, or installation advice. Bathroom modifications and support products should match the person, the home, and the surface where they are used or installed.

## First things to check

- Is the path to the bathroom clear and well lit?
- Is the bathroom floor frequently wet or cluttered?
- Is the tub or shower surface slippery?
- Does the person hold towel bars, counters, shower doors, or nearby furniture for support?
- Are toiletries reachable without bending, twisting, or standing on tiptoe?
- Is the toilet height manageable?
- Is there a reliable way to call for help from the bathroom?

## Shower and tub checklist

- Review whether the person can step in and out safely.
- Check whether the surface feels slippery when wet.
- Keep soap, shampoo, towels, and other items within easy reach.
- Avoid clutter around the tub or shower edge.
- Discuss shower chairs or transfer benches with a qualified professional if bathing feels unstable.
- Look for stable seating with nonskid leg tips when shower seating is recommended.
- Review product weight limits, instructions, and fit before using any bathroom safety product.

## Grab bar caution

Grab bars can be helpful when properly selected and installed, but they are not interchangeable with towel bars, decorative fixtures, or shower doors. Do not assume an existing fixture, suction product, or temporary support can safely hold body weight.

If grab bars may be needed, consider professional guidance on placement, wall structure, installation method, and the person's actual movement pattern. A misplaced support may not help at the moment it is needed most.

## Toilet area checklist

- Check whether sitting down and standing up feels difficult.
- Check whether the person reaches for unstable objects for support.
- Review whether surrounding space is clear enough for mobility aids.
- Consider whether toilet-height changes or nearby support should be discussed with a professional.
- Keep toilet paper, hygiene supplies, and call devices within reasonable reach.

## Nighttime bathroom trips

Nighttime bathroom trips deserve special attention because the person may be tired, rushed, groggy, or moving in low light.

- Add reachable lighting near the bed.
- Keep the route to the bathroom clear.
- Reduce loose rugs, cords, and clutter along the path.
- Consider motion lighting if the person gets up at night.
- Keep mobility aids in a consistent location.
- Make sure glasses, hearing aids, slippers, phone, or alert device are easy to find.

## What not to do

- Do not treat towel bars as grab bars.
- Do not assume a suction product is safe for body-weight support.
- Do not buy products before understanding the person's movement pattern.
- Do not ignore a bathroom near-fall because no one was injured.
- Do not make major changes without checking whether they fit the actual bathroom layout and the person's needs.

## When to ask for professional help

Consider professional help if:

- There has been a recent fall or near-fall in or near the bathroom.
- The person struggles to step into the tub or shower.
- The person needs to pull on fixtures for support.
- A shower chair, transfer bench, grab bar, raised toilet seat, or major modification is being considered.
- There are memory, judgment, dizziness, weakness, medication, or vision concerns affecting bathroom routines.
- The bathroom is narrow, cluttered, or difficult to modify.
- Family members disagree about whether the current setup is safe.

Potential helpers may include a primary care clinician, pharmacist, occupational therapist, physical therapist, certified aging-in-place specialist, licensed contractor, or local aging-services agency depending on the problem.
